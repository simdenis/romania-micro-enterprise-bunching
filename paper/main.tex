\begin{abstract}
\noindent Romanian companies with revenue below a ceiling set in euro pay 1 to 3 percent of revenue instead of 16 percent of profit. The ceiling moved six times between 2013 and 2026, which gives a counterfactual density at each notch from years when no ceiling was there, and repeated reassignment of the same firms between a revenue base and a profit base. In the accounts of every company, 9.6 million firm-years, reported revenue piles up just below the ceiling, follows it when it moves, and within a fixed euro ceiling tracks its lei value at the previous year-end exchange rate. The pile-up is 2 to 8 percent of nearby firms and 0.06 to 0.30 percent of all firms. It is concentrated among firms whose margin the year before put them on the gaining side of the notch, but firms that reported a loss bunch too, which points to compliance cost. A ceiling enacted in October 2015 for the following year produced within two months a spike just above it, of low-margin firms that then left a regime that was mandatory. Firms moved onto the revenue base by a ceiling increase raise their reported margins, and firms moved off it lower them by 8 to 17 percent of the margin, so the fiscal cost of the regime and the revenue gain from narrowing it are both smaller than static estimates imply.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Many countries tax small firms on their revenue and larger firms on their profit \citep{sharma2025dual, kanbur2014, dharmapala2011}. The size limit between the two regimes is a notch: crossing it switches the tax base and, for a firm whose margin is above the ratio of the two rates, raises the tax bill at once. Responses to such notches have been measured at VAT thresholds in the United Kingdom \citep{liu2021vat}, Finland \citep{harju2019vat} and Spain \citep{almunia2018}, and at turnover-tax and corporate-tax cut-offs in Pakistan \citep{kleven2013}, Armenia \citep{asatryan2016}, South Africa \citep{boonzaaier2019} and Costa Rica \citep{bachas2021}; the choice between the two bases is the subject of \citet{best2015}. In all of these settings the limit is fixed or moves rarely, so the density that would exist without the notch is extrapolated from the density away from it, usually with a polynomial, and \citet{blomquist2021} show that the result depends on restrictions the data cannot check. \citet{best2018} compare a location before and after a notch was introduced; \citet{marx2024} uses panel variation.

Romania's limit moves. The micro-enterprise regime taxes revenue at 1 to 3 percent for companies whose annual revenue is below a euro ceiling, and the ceiling changed six times: \euro{}65,000 until 2015, \euro{}100,000 in 2016, \euro{}500,000 in 2017, \euro{}1,000,000 from 2018 to 2022, \euro{}500,000 in 2023 and 2024, \euro{}250,000 in 2025 and \euro{}100,000 from 2026. That variation does two things. Every location that was a notch in one year was an ordinary point of the distribution in others, which supplies a counterfactual without a fitted curve. And every move reassigned a band of firms between a revenue base and a profit base without any action of their own, which lets us see what the base does to what firms report.

The paper's results are these. The pile-up in reported revenue follows the ceiling up and down, and within a fixed euro ceiling it sits within a few thousand lei of the ceiling's value at the previous year-end exchange rate and moves with it; a spike at 4,779,300 lei that moves to 4,869,400 lei the next year is not a rounding habit. Measured against the same quantile in ceiling-free years, the excess is 2 to 8 percent of the firms within 20 percent of the ceiling, robust to windows, control sets and bootstraps, and centred on zero at placebo locations; in aggregate it is 0.06 to 0.30 percent of firms. Bunchers are high-margin firms in professional, administrative, health and information services, and the bunching comes mostly from firms whose margin the year before put them on the gaining side of the notch. A ceiling enacted in October 2015 for the following year produced, within two months, a spike just above it, of low-margin firms that then left a regime that was mandatory at the time. When the 2018 increase moved firms onto the revenue base their reported margins rose relative to firms left on profit tax; when the 2023 cut moved them back, margins fell by 1 to 2 points, 8 to 17 percent of the margin. Because 81 percent of the revenue at stake in the regime comes from firms below \euro{}500,000, and because reported profit falls when firms move to the profit base, both the cost of the regime and the gain from narrowing it are smaller than static estimates imply. A 2023 rule requiring one employee produced about 20,000 one-employee firms in the year it was enacted and pushed the rest out of the regime.

Section~\ref{sec:conc} concludes.

\section{Institutional background}\label{sec:inst}

The micro-enterprise income tax (\emph{impozitul pe veniturile microîntreprinderilor}), Title III of the Fiscal Code, replaces the 16 percent profit tax with a tax on revenue for companies whose revenue in the previous fiscal year was below a ceiling set in euro and that meet side conditions. Table~\ref{tab:regime} gives both by year of revenue; Appendix~\ref{app:legal} gives the acts.

Three features matter. The first is timing. Under the in-year rule, a micro-enterprise whose cumulated revenue passes the ceiling during year $t$ owes profit tax from that quarter (for 2013 to 2015, for the whole year), the ceiling converted at the central bank (BNR) rate at the close of year $t-1$. Under the year-end rule, status for year $t+1$ depends on revenue at 31 December of year $t$ at the rate at the close of year $t$. When the ceiling is unchanged the two rules put the notch a few thousand lei apart and the in-year rule, at the earlier and lower rate, binds. When a cut has been enacted for the following year a firm faces two notches. That happened in 2022, when Ordinance 16/2022 of July set \euro{}500,000 for 2023 status on 2022 revenue and Law 370/2022 of 20 December deferred the revenue test to 2023 revenue; and in 2025, when Ordinance 156/2024 of 31 December 2024 set \euro{}250,000 for 2025 and \euro{}100,000 for 2026 status on 2025 revenue. A 2016 ceiling of \euro{}100,000, enacted in October 2015 and tested on 2015 revenue, is a further year-end notch that we return to below.

The second feature is the tax base. The ceiling is tested on the taxable base of Article 53, which leaves out items such as subsidies, provision reversals and, from 2021, dividends received. The accounts report total revenue, which is the same or larger; where the excluded items are not zero a firm looks closer to the ceiling than it is, which shifts the bunching region slightly to the right in our data.

The third is the side conditions. The employee condition is met by a full-time contract, part-time contracts adding up to one, or a paid mandate contract; linked enterprises are defined by holdings above 25 percent; the activities taxed at 3 percent regardless of revenue in 2024 and 2025 are software, hospitality, legal and medical services. The consulting cap, in force in 2014--2017 and, reformulated, in 2023--2024, is a margin the accounts do not show. The VAT exemption for small firms is a separate notch in lei, 220,000 lei until March 2018, 300,000 from 1 April 2018 and 395,000 from 1 September 2025, which Appendix~\ref{app:vat} treats.

\section{Data}\label{sec:data}

The Ministry of Finance publishes, for each fiscal year, the main figures from the annual accounts of every company, identified by fiscal code (CUI) and CAEN activity code: balance-sheet totals, net turnover, total revenue, total expenses, gross and net profit or loss, and average headcount. The layout is stable from 2016; the 2014 and 2015 files use an older set of columns, which we harmonise. The 2021, 2023 and 2025 files were published a few weeks after the filing deadline and the others are later re-issues, so late filers are covered unevenly. Table~\ref{tab:panel} summarises the panel: 9.58 million firm-years after removing 32 duplicates, 1.56 million firms. Total revenue is the variable for the micro-enterprise notch.

The accounts do not say which regime a firm is in. We recover it in two ways. Gross profit minus net profit is the income tax accrued; divided by revenue it is roughly 1, 2 or 3 percent for micro-enterprises, and divided by pre-tax profit it is roughly 16 percent for profit-tax firms. The ANAF taxpayer registry, a snapshot dated 31 August 2023, has one column per tax a firm is registered for. Table~\ref{tab:crosscheck} cross-tabulates the two for 2023: of firms registered for the micro-enterprise tax, 84 percent show a micro pattern and 1 percent a profit-tax pattern; of firms registered for profit tax, 22 percent show the profit-tax pattern and half paid no tax. Where both are decisive, 57 percent of firms, they agree in 92.5 percent of cases. The inference picks out micro-enterprises well and profit-tax firms poorly, so we use the registry flag where membership matters and the accounts-based measure as a check.

\section{Empirical strategy}\label{sec:method}

For each year $t$ the notch location is $L_t = T_t \, e_{t-1}$, where $T_t$ is the euro ceiling for revenue in year $t$ and $e_{t-1}$ the BNR rate at the close of year $t-1$. Let $F_t$ be the distribution of revenue in year $t$ and $q_t = F_t(L_t)$ the quantile of the notch; for every other year $c$ let $L^*_c = F_c^{-1}(q_t)$ be the revenue at the same quantile. Year $c$ is an admissible control if no known notch of year $c$, the micro ceiling, an enacted or announced future ceiling, the \euro{}60,000 rate boundary, or the VAT threshold, lies within 25 percent of $L^*_c$. Bins are 1 percent of the location wide in a window of $\pm$20 percent; shares $s_{t,k}$ are bin counts divided by the count in the window, and the counterfactual share is the mean of $s_{c,k}$ over admissible controls. Excess mass is
\begin{equation}
b_t = \frac{\sum_{k \in B} \left( s_{t,k} - \bar{s}_{c,k} \right)}{\frac{1}{|B|}\sum_{k\in B} \bar{s}_{c,k}},
\end{equation}
with $B$ the five bins just below the notch; $m_t$ is the same sum over the five bins just above. With 1 percent bins $b$ is roughly the width of the bunching region in percent of the ceiling. The identifying assumption is that the shape of the revenue density around a given quantile is the same across years apart from the notch. Quantile matching makes the counterfactual immune to inflation and to growth in firm size, not to changes in shape, so we test it: the estimator is run at 74 placebo locations, at 0.45 to 2.5 times each year's ceiling and at least 25 percent from any notch, where the true excess is zero. The notch list includes the year-end ceilings enacted for the following year, one of which, the \euro{}100,000 ceiling for 2016 tested on 2015 revenue, we found because an earlier placebo grid built without it had its largest value at that location. Because shares are normalised within the window, the excess and missing mass offset by construction; we therefore also compute a count-based version in which each bin is a share of all firms in the year, $N_t$, so that the integration constraint becomes a test of where bunchers come from. Uncertainty is reported three ways: a multinomial bootstrap of bin counts, a block bootstrap that resamples control years, and the placebo distribution. Appendix Table~\ref{tab:controls} lists every control year with its matched revenue and distance to the nearest notch; the closest cases are 26 to 30 percent, for the 2014--2016 notches and the two year-end notches, and tightening the rule to 35 percent changes the estimates from 2016 on by at most 0.01.

Three things the estimator does not deliver are stated once here. It does not locate the missing mass, which is spread over the window above the notch rather than concentrated next to it, so the standard conversion of excess mass to an elasticity has no anchor. The tax change on crossing depends on each firm's margin, because the base switches, so a representative-firm elasticity has no clear meaning. And reported margins themselves respond to the base, as Section~\ref{sec:base} shows. Appendix Table~\ref{tab:elasticity} reports the Kleven--Waseem reduced-form elasticity under two proxies for the bunching width, which differ by three orders of magnitude; we report excess mass and excess shares instead.

\subsection{The spike follows the ceiling and the exchange rate}

Figure~\ref{fig:panels} shows a spike in the bin just below the ceiling in every year: at \euro{}65,000 in 2014 and 2015, \euro{}100,000 in 2016, \euro{}500,000 in 2017, \euro{}1,000,000 from 2018 to 2022, \euro{}500,000 in 2023 and 2024 and \euro{}250,000 in 2025. Figure~\ref{fig:lei} looks inside the five years at \euro{}1,000,000, when the ceiling's lei value rose from 4.66 to 4.95 million as the leu depreciated. The spike moves with it each year; Table~\ref{tab:lei} puts the modal 5,000-lei bin 1,400 to 5,600 lei below the ceiling at the previous year-end rate, the rate the in-year rule prescribes, while the ceiling at the current year-end rate is 79,000 to 115,000 lei further right in 2019 to 2021. Nobody rounds revenue to 4,779,300 lei, and a spike at that value that moves to 4,869,400 lei the next year is not an accounting convention.

\subsection{Difference-in-bunching estimates}

Table~\ref{tab:dib} reports the estimates and Figure~\ref{fig:dib} the densities for four notches; all fifteen are in Appendix Figure~\ref{fig:dibfull}. In every year the treated density exceeds the same-quantile control density in the bins just below the notch, and the control density is smooth through it. Excess mass in the 5 percent below the notch ranges from 1.9 percent of firms in the window, at the \euro{}500,000 ceiling of 2017 and the \euro{}250,000 ceiling of 2025, to 7.6 percent at the \euro{}500,000 ceiling of 2023. As a share of all filers it is 0.06 to 0.30 percent. Table~\ref{tab:robust} shows that a block bootstrap over control years gives standard errors of 0.08 to 0.16, that dropping any one control year moves $b$ by at most 0.1, and that narrowing the window to $\pm$10 percent lowers $b$ by 15 to 36 percent and the excess count by 12 to 31 percent, while widening it to $\pm$30 percent raises both by about a tenth or less, except in 2014 where the VAT spike enters the wider window. That dependence is mechanical: shares are normalised within the window, so a narrower window deflates every treated share by the bunchers' own weight in it, and the count-based excess of Table~\ref{tab:countbased}, which does not use the window, is identical to within three firms at all three widths; Appendix Table~\ref{tab:robustextra} adds that control years before and after the treated year agree, that correcting the control quantile for the bunchers changes $b$ by at most 0.1, and that continuing firms give $b$ lower by 0.05 to 0.2.

The placebo distribution is the relevant benchmark. Across the 74 locations $b$ has mean 0.01 and standard deviation 0.13; the 95th and 99th percentiles of $|b|$ are 0.27 and 0.34 and the maximum is 0.39. Every in-year estimate exceeds that maximum, most by a factor of two or more; the 2017 and 2025 in-year estimates, 0.76 and 0.73, are the weakest, and in the count-based version, where the placebo maximum is 0.61, they are 0.94 and 1.00, only one and a half times it. Two things dilute the 2025 notch: the enacted \euro{}100,000 ceiling for 2026 competed for attention, and at the 3 percent rate then in force the break-even margin was above the median buncher's. A within-year placebo points the same way as the grid: hospitality firms, exempt from the ceiling in 2023, show an excess of under 1 percent of their window that year against 7.6 percent for all firms (Table~\ref{tab:sector}).

Table~\ref{tab:countbased} gives the count-based version. At every large notch the treated year has more firms than the counterfactual across the whole 20 percent below the notch, not only in the five bins next to it, and a deficit in the 20 percent above of at most a seventh of the excess; the net difference over the window is 10 to 17 percent of the window for the 2019 to 2024 notches, against a placebo 95th percentile of 5 percent. Bunchers are therefore not drawn from the region just above the ceiling, and the width of the bunching region, and any elasticity derived from it, is not identified in these data. Where the extra firms come from is less settled. Three candidates: firms below the ceiling grow less, so the density below it accumulates over the years the ceiling is in place, the build-up that \citet{kleven2013} document for Pakistan and that shows here as a rise from 3.5 percent of the window in 2018 to 5.5 percent in 2021; new and split firms enter the window, 11 percent of the 2023 excess being firms in their first year; or the shape assumption holds in shares but not in counts. Two checks are in Appendix Tables~\ref{tab:countwide} and \ref{tab:growth}. Extending the window to $\pm$50 percent finds, for 2019 to 2024, a surplus of 0 to 5 percent of the counterfactual in the first 10 percent above the notch, deficits of 1 to 8 percent in the 10 to 30 percent above and nothing systematic further out, but also a surplus of 2 to 8 percent from 20 to 50 percent below, further than a bunching response reaches, and for the 2014 to 2016 notches a deficit of 11 to 17 percent there; the count-based comparison picks up differences in the shape of the distribution across years as well as the notch, which is what the window normalisation removes. The growth check is closer to the mechanism. In every notch year in which the ceiling was unchanged the following year, firms 5 to 20 percent below the notch grew less from $t$ to $t+1$, relative to firms 20 to 35 percent below, than the same bands did in control years, by 0.3 to 2.6 points, and in four of the six years the share crossing the next year's notch was 3 to 9 points lower. Slower growth below the ceiling is there, but it is small, and the frictions reading of \citet{kleven2013}, a region above the notch where most firms do not move, fits the flat density above it as well. We read the surplus as partly accumulation and partly shape drift, and the conclusions do not rest on it.

Two further features of Table~\ref{tab:dib}. The 2023 response is the largest and falls to 4.1 percent in 2024 at the same ceiling; the ceiling halved and exit became permanent that year. The share of 2023 bunchers that had been above \euro{}500,000 in 2022, 29.6 percent, is no higher relative to a comparison group than in years when that point was not a ceiling (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:transition}), so it is mean reversion, not displaced firms requalifying. And the three extra rows show year-end notches operating, each with a different timing. In 2015 the \euro{}100,000 ceiling for 2016 status, enacted in October 2015 with two months of the year left, produced no excess below its lei value at the end-2015 rate, 452,450 lei, but an excess of 0.71 (standard error 0.08) in the five bins above it, about 500 firms or 1.7 percent of the window, most of them in the first two bins; the 2014 distribution is flat there. The regime was mandatory, so a firm below the ceiling that expected to lose from a 1 to 3 percent tax on revenue in 2016 could avoid it only by having 2015 revenue above the ceiling. Appendix Table~\ref{tab:escape2015} follows the two sides of the ceiling into 2016. Firms 0 to 3 percent above it were on profit tax the next year, 21 percent with a profit-tax pattern and 32 percent paying no tax, against 9 and 13 percent for firms 0 to 5 percent below, of which 57 percent show the micro pattern;\footnote{A fifth of the firms above the ceiling also show a micro pattern in 2016, when the law put them on profit tax. We read this as inference noise rather than as Article 53 exclusions moving their taxable base below the ceiling: in the 2014 cohort at the same quantile, where every firm was above the \euro{}65,000 ceiling and on profit tax, 14 to 15 percent show the same pattern, because a profit-tax firm with a margin of 6 to 19 percent pays 16 percent of profit, which is 1 to 3 percent of revenue, the signature the inference reads as the revenue tax. The exclusions may account for the remaining few points.} the median tax paid in 2016 was 0.5 percent of revenue above the ceiling and 1.0 percent below. The firms in the spike have low margins, a median of 3.8 percent with 60 percent below the 6.25 percent break-even (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:yearend2015}), so they gained from being above. What the panel does not show is a reversal: their 2016 revenue grew by a median 4.2 percent, no less than the 2.5 percent of firms 3 to 10 percent above, and the share that fell back below the ceiling's value, 42 percent, is within a few points of that group and of the 2014 placebo cohort. One detail of the location supports the reading. At the rates visible while the year was still open, monthly averages of 4.42 lei in October, 4.44 in November and 4.50 in December 2015, \euro{}100,000 was worth 442,000 to 450,000 lei; the spike does not start there but at 452,000, just above the end-year value of 452,450 that fixed the test. Firms cleared the ceiling at its year-end value, whether by leaving a margin for the rate or by placing revenue once the rate was known, and neither round-number nor fixed-lei targeting produces a spike that begins at 452,450 lei. The spike is firms that placed themselves on the profit-tax side of a ceiling they expected to lose from and stayed there; whether the placement was a timing of real revenue or a reporting choice the data cannot tell. In 2022 a spike of 2.0 percent of the window appears at \euro{}500,000, the ceiling firms expected for 2023 status until the December deferral, with none at that location in 2021. In 2025 a spike of 3.7 percent appears at \euro{}100,000, the ceiling enacted for 2026. That spike runs from 494,000 to 500,000 lei at 1,000-lei resolution, straddling the \euro{}100,000 value at the end-2024 rate, 497,410 lei, which is why $m$ is positive for that row; the data cannot separate targeting of the last observable rate from targeting of 500,000 lei as a round number. For comparison, a polynomial counterfactual of the kind used by \citet{chetty2011}, at bin widths of 1.5 to 2 percent of the ceiling and with the VAT spike inside the 2014 and 2015 windows, gives 0.4 to 2.0 at baseline and, across 27 combinations of bin width, degree and excluded window, anything from negative values to more than twice the baseline; the two sets of estimates are not on the same scale and the comparison is only about stability.

\subsection{Who bunches}

The notch raises taxes only for firms whose margin exceeds the ratio of the two rates, 6.25 percent at a 1 percent revenue tax and 18.75 percent at 3 percent. Table~\ref{tab:margins} compares firms in the 5 percent below the ceiling with firms just above it, in the notch year and in the year before. The year before matters because Section~\ref{sec:base} shows that the base changes what firms report: firms just above the ceiling in year $t$ crossed it during the year and closed their accounts on profit tax, so part of any gap in year-$t$ margins is the base effect rather than selection. In $t-1$ the two groups were on more similar bases, in 2017 and 2018 nearly all on profit tax because the previous ceiling was lower, and the gap survives: bunchers' median margins in $t-1$ are 7 to 20 percent against 3 to 12 percent for firms just above, and the share above the break-even margin in $t-1$ is 40 to 76 percent against 23 to 66. Table~\ref{tab:sector} shows where the bunchers are: in 2023, excess mass is 11 to 15 percent of the window in professional, administrative, health and real-estate services and in construction, and 10 percent in information services, against 5 percent in trade, 3.5 in transport and 1 in agriculture, and bunchers in the service sectors report margins of 29 to 43 percent.

Table~\ref{tab:dibmargin} makes the split the unit of estimation. Firms are divided by their $t-1$ margin into those above the break-even margin for the rate at the ceiling and those at or below it, and the estimator is run within each group, each matched at its own quantile in the same control years. Bunching is concentrated where the tax wedge is: among firms that gain, $b$ is 1.3 to 4.4 and the excess 3 to 11 percent of the window; among firms with a positive margin at or below break-even, $b$ is 0 to 1.3 and the excess 0 to 3 percent. This resolves the puzzle of the 3 percent years, in which the median buncher's margin, 13 to 17 percent, is below the 18.75 percent at which a 3 percent revenue tax saves tax, yet 2014 and 2015 show excess mass of 1.2 and 1.7. In 2015 the gaining group, a quarter of firms, has $b$ of 4.4 and an excess of 11 percent of its window, the low-positive group 1.3 and 3 percent; in 2024 and 2025 the figures are 3.4 and 1.5 against 0.6 and 0.2. The median hides a mix, and most of the bunching comes from the minority that gains. The third group is the informative one. Firms that reported a loss in $t-1$, for whom crossing the ceiling removes the tax altogether, bunch more than firms with a small positive margin: $b$ of 0.3 to 2.0 and an excess of 0.8 to 5 percent of the window, two or more standard errors from zero in nine of eleven years. Some of them will have expected a profit their previous year did not show, but for the rest no tax wedge holds them below the ceiling, and this is the cleanest evidence in the paper for the mechanism \citet{harju2019vat} find at the Finnish VAT threshold, compliance cost: leaving the regime meant, in those years, moving from a one-line tax base to quarterly profit computations, the deductibility rules of the profit tax and the audit exposure that comes with them. In 2016 the rate depended on headcount and the median buncher paid 1 percent, so 2016 is not a 3 percent year.

\section{The base changes what firms report}\label{sec:base}

A micro-enterprise's tax does not depend on its reported profit; a profit-tax firm's does. Firms on profit tax therefore have a reason to report lower margins, by booking costs aggressively, or by paying the owner a salary, which is deductible under profit tax and taxed on the owner, rather than a dividend, which is not deductible and was taxed at 5 to 8 percent. Both responses lower reported profit; only the first is misreporting. The accounts do not separate personnel costs, so we cannot tell them apart and say so; what we can do is measure the total.

\subsection{Design}

In 2018 the ceiling rose from \euro{}500,000 to \euro{}1,000,000, so firms with 2017 revenue between those values were moved from profit tax to revenue tax without doing anything, while firms above \euro{}1,000,000 stayed on profit tax. In 2023 the cut reversed the move. Wide bands, (\euro{}500,000, \euro{}1,000,000] against (\euro{}1,000,000, \euro{}2,000,000], compare firms that differ twofold in size and produce mean-reversion drift of up to 1.5 points in a placebo (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:eventstudy}). We therefore use bands 20 percent either side of the cut-off, assigned on two-year average revenue, in balanced panels, and placebos built at the same time with the same bands at \euro{}1,500,000 and \euro{}2,000,000, where no regime changed. Table~\ref{tab:firststage} gives the first stage. In 2018 the treated band's micro share, inferred from the accounts, jumps from 15 to 55 percent; the control band just above the new ceiling goes from 15 to 29 percent, because some of those firms bunched under it, so the control is partly treated, which is why the entry effect fades over the horizon in the baseline. A control band of 1.2 to 1.5 times the cut-off stays at 15 to 25 percent. The exit design is fuzzier and its first stage is not well measured. The inference classifies 42 percent of the control band as micro in 2022, above the ceiling, because firms that cross the ceiling during the year pay both taxes and show a blended ratio. The ANAF registry, which records the regime a firm is registered for, puts the treated band at 40 percent micro in August 2023 and 6 percent in June 2024, against 12 and 2 percent for the control band, so by 2024 both bands were almost entirely on profit tax and the treated band had lost the regime on a scale the inference understates. We therefore report the exit effect as an intention-to-treat estimate on the band that lost the regime and compute per-switch effects for the entry design only.

\subsection{The employee condition}

From 1 January 2023 a micro-enterprise must have at least one employee, tested at 31 December 2022; the rule was enacted in July 2022. Table~\ref{tab:emp} shows the share of small firms with exactly one employee rising from 31.9 percent in 2021 to 37.6 in 2022 and 40.7 in 2023. Table~\ref{tab:emptrans} follows zero-employee firms: 22.3 percent of them moved to exactly one employee in 2022, against 11.1 to 11.3 percent in the two preceding transitions, or 20.3 percent when the sample is limited to firms present since 2020; among zero-employee firms above \euro{}1,500,000, untouched by the rule, the rate stayed at 5 to 6 percent. That is about 20,000 additional one-employee firms. Of micro-enterprises still without employees at the end of 2022, 82 percent were registered for profit tax in 2023 and 15 percent had an employee by the end of 2023. The hiring came in the enactment year and the rest left the regime. The aggregate shows the scale. ANAF's count of taxpayers registered for the micro-enterprise tax fell from 912,875 at 31 December 2022 to 495,448 at 31 December 2023 and 368,900 at 31 December 2024 \citep[Table 2.2]{anaf2024, anaf2025}: the 2023 reform, the employee rule together with the halved ceiling, the three-firm rule and the consulting cap, took 46 percent of registrants out of the regime in one year, and the 2024 rules a further 26 percent. The registry counts registrations, not filers, so it includes dormant firms and firms that never filed accounts, which is why it exceeds the 862,564 companies with 2022 accounts in our panel; the two series are not the same population, but the fall is the counterpart of Table~\ref{tab:emptrans}. The implied tax rates of the 2022 hirers suggest many hired late in the year, but rate inference is noisy for one-employee firms, so we do not lean on the timing. Headcount counts a part-time contract as one, so we read this as compliance at the lowest cost rather than job creation.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conc}

A ceiling that moved six times gives a counterfactual density without a fitted curve and reassigns the same firms between a revenue base and a profit base. The first shows that bunching at the ceiling is real, follows the ceiling and the exchange rate, and amounts to 2 to 8 percent of nearby firms and 0.06 to 0.30 percent of all firms, with no deficit just above the ceiling; firms just below it grow a little less than firms further down, so part of the response is an accumulation below the ceiling, and where the rest of the surplus comes from the data do not settle. The second shows that reported margins rise when firms are moved onto the revenue base and fall by 8 to 17 percent of the margin when they are moved off it. Together they say that both the fiscal cost of the regime and the revenue from narrowing it are smaller than static estimates imply: 81 percent of the cost sits with firms well below the ceiling, and the profit those firms would report under profit tax is lower than the profit they report now.

Three design points follow, limited to the margins we measure. The ceiling is a lever on the intramarginal population, not on behaviour: moving it changes the behaviour of a few thousand firms and the bills of hundreds of thousands. The regime's advantage is concentrated in high-margin services, and a structure that ties the rate to the margin, the minimum-tax design that \citet{sharma2025dual} show dominates threshold systems, would remove both the notch and the incentive to report margins differently on its two sides. Side conditions that can be met with a single contract produce that contract, as the employee rule did; and side conditions together with a halved ceiling removed 46 percent of registered micro-enterprises in a single year, 2023, a change in the regime's population larger than anything the ceiling alone has done. What the data do not settle is how much of the margin response is misreporting and how much is income shifting between salary and dividends; that needs personnel costs, which the public accounts do not carry.

\section{Data details}\label{app:data}

\paragraph{Regime inference.} The gap between gross and net profit also contains the specific tax on hospitality firms in 2017--2022 and other taxes on the result, net of sponsorship and cash-register credits, so ratios such as 0.8 or 2.4 percent occur and a firm with positive tax and a pre-tax loss is most likely, but not certainly, a micro-enterprise. Of firms registered for profit tax, 8 percent show a micro pattern and half paid no tax, which is what loss-making and dormant profit-tax firms look like; 2.6 percent of 2023 filers are not in the registry. In the registry's column dictionary, obligation 120 is the micro-enterprise income tax and obligation 100 the profit tax.

\paragraph{Employees.} Headcount is reported as zero for a further 5 percent of small firms beyond the blanks. Of firms that go from zero to one employee in 2022, 12 percent paid the no-employee rate for most of that year, which a true average would have rounded to zero; late-year hires are therefore counted. The legal test is met by a full-time contract, by part-time contracts adding up to one, or by a paid mandate contract, none of which the field distinguishes.

\paragraph{Trade register.} The register lists administrators as of the snapshot date, not shareholders and not past administrators; the open data has a representatives table only in snapshots from November 2025 onwards, so administrators at the time of the 2018--2023 observations cannot be recovered. Company names carry no usable marker of sole ownership. Nearly all filers, 96 to 99 percent by year, link to at least one administrator, and the linked share is the same across the groups compared. Four percent of administrator records lack a birth date, so their key is the name alone; results are reported with and without them. Names, birth dates and addresses are used only to build hashed keys and appear in no output or replication file. Net turnover, used for the VAT threshold, is blank for a fifth of micro-entity forms but for under 1 percent of firms near that threshold.

\section{The VAT threshold and the rate boundary}\label{app:vat}

The VAT threshold moved from 300,000 to 395,000 lei on 1 September 2025. In the 2025 distribution of turnover the spike at 300,000 lei is gone and a spike at 395,000 has replaced it, with polynomial excess mass of 3.0 against 0.6 at that location in 2024 (Table~\ref{tab:vat}); part of the spike is mechanical, since the ordinance let firms that crossed 300,000 lei in August 2025 stay unregistered up to the new threshold. The difference-in-bunching estimator is not available at this threshold because a VAT notch exists in every year within 25 percent of the same-quantile location. The polynomial estimates in Table~\ref{tab:vat} and Figure~\ref{fig:vat} show excess mass between 2.8 and 6.6 in every year from 2014 to 2024 at bin widths of 0.7 to 0.9 percent of the threshold, against 0.4 to 2.0 for the micro-enterprise notch at bin widths of 1.5 to 2 percent. The legal base is the turnover of Article 310(2), which differs from net turnover by exempt and ancillary items. In 2024 the \euro{}60,000 rate boundary and the VAT threshold are 1,500 lei apart and the combined spike has polynomial excess mass of 4.9; in 2025, with the VAT threshold at 395,000 lei, a spike remains at 296,000 to 298,000 lei, the lei value of \euro{}60,000 at the end-2024 rate, with excess mass of 0.9 (Table~\ref{tab:boundary60k}, Figure~\ref{fig:boundary60k}). The residual spike is where the rate boundary predicts it. Among micro-enterprises outside the listed activities the 296,000 to 298,000 lei bin holds 67 percent more firms than the neighbouring bins and the 298,000 to 300,000 bin 40 percent more; among micro-enterprises in the listed activities, taxed at 3 percent regardless of revenue, and among profit-tax firms, neither bin is elevated (Table~\ref{tab:boundary60kregime}). Firms subject to the rate boundary respond to it and only they do, but the response is a fifth the size of the VAT response; the activity list already puts software, legal and medical firms on 3 percent regardless of revenue, and those are the high-margin firms for which two points of revenue matter most.
