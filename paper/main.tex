\begin{abstract}
\noindent Romanian companies with revenue below a ceiling set in euro pay 1 to 3 percent of revenue instead of 16 percent of profit. The ceiling moved six times between 2013 and 2026. Each move gives a counterfactual density at the notch from years when no ceiling was there, and each move reassigned the same firms between a revenue base and a profit base. The data are the annual accounts of every company, 9.6 million firm-years. Reported revenue piles up just below the ceiling and moves with it; in years when the euro ceiling is unchanged, the pile-up sits at the ceiling's lei value at the previous year-end exchange rate and shifts as that rate does. The pile-up is 2 to 8 percent of nearby firms and 0.06 to 0.30 percent of all firms. It comes mostly from firms whose margin in the previous year put them on the gaining side of the notch, but firms that reported a loss bunch too, which points to compliance cost. A ceiling enacted in October 2015 for the following year produced a spike just above it within two months; the firms in the spike had low margins and then left a regime that was mandatory. Firms moved onto the revenue base by a ceiling increase raise their reported margins. Firms moved off it lower their median margin by 8 to 17 percent. Both the fiscal cost of the regime and the revenue gain from narrowing it are therefore smaller than static estimates imply.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Dozens of countries tax small firms on their revenue and larger firms on their profit \citep{sharma2025dual, kanbur2014, dharmapala2011}. \citet{best2015} set out the trade-off. A revenue base is harder to evade and cheaper to administer, but it taxes costs. A profit base does not tax costs, but it leaves the profit figure to the firm. Two questions have stayed open since. First, what does the base do to what firms report? Do firms on profit tax report lower margins than the same firms would report on revenue tax? Second, where does the fiscal cost of a revenue-tax regime sit? Is it with the firms that arrange their revenue to stay under the size limit, or with the intramarginal population that would be inside the regime anyway? The first question needs the same firms observed on both bases without having chosen either. The second needs a measure of behaviour at the limit that does not rest on an assumed shape of the firm-size distribution. The size limit between the regimes is a notch. Crossing it switches the base, and for a firm whose margin is above the ratio of the two rates it raises the tax bill at once. Responses to such notches have been measured at VAT thresholds in the United Kingdom \citep{liu2021vat}, Finland \citep{harju2019vat} and Spain \citep{almunia2018}, and at turnover-tax and corporate-tax cut-offs in Pakistan \citep{kleven2013}, Armenia \citep{asatryan2016}, South Africa \citep{boonzaaier2019} and Costa Rica \citep{bachas2021}. In all of these settings the limit is fixed or moves rarely. The density that would exist without the notch is therefore extrapolated from the density away from it, usually with a polynomial, and \citet{blomquist2021} show that the result depends on restrictions the data cannot check. \citet{best2018} compare a location before and after a notch was introduced; \citet{marx2024} uses panel variation.

Romania's limit moves, and that is what lets us answer both questions. The micro-enterprise regime taxes revenue at 1 to 3 percent for companies whose annual revenue is below a euro ceiling. The ceiling changed six times: \euro{}65,000 until 2015, \euro{}100,000 in 2016, \euro{}500,000 in 2017, \euro{}1,000,000 from 2018 to 2022, \euro{}500,000 in 2023 and 2024, \euro{}250,000 in 2025 and \euro{}100,000 from 2026. Every move reassigned a band of firms between a revenue base and a profit base without any action of their own. That is the experiment the first question needs. Every location that was a notch in one year was an ordinary point of the distribution in other years. That supplies a counterfactual without a fitted curve, and so a measure of behaviour at the limit for the second question.

The paper's results are these. The pile-up in reported revenue follows the ceiling up and down. In years when the euro ceiling is unchanged, the pile-up sits within a few thousand lei of the ceiling's value at the previous year-end exchange rate and moves as that rate does; a spike at 4,779,300 lei that moves to 4,869,400 lei the next year is not a rounding habit. Measured against the same quantile in ceiling-free years, the excess is 2 to 8 percent of the firms within 20 percent of the ceiling. It is robust to windows, control sets and bootstraps, and it is centred on zero at placebo locations. In aggregate it is 0.06 to 0.30 percent of firms. Who bunches shows that this is a tax response. Bunchers are high-margin firms in professional, administrative, health and information services, and most of the bunching comes from firms whose margin in the previous year put them on the gaining side of the notch. A ceiling enacted in October 2015 for the following year produced a spike just above it within two months; the firms in the spike had low margins and then left a regime that was mandatory at the time. A 2023 rule requiring one employee produced about 20,000 one-employee firms in the year it was enacted and pushed the rest out of the regime. When the 2018 increase moved firms onto the revenue base, their reported margins rose relative to firms left on profit tax. When the 2023 cut moved them back, margins fell by 1 to 2 points, 8 to 17 percent of the margin. Firms below \euro{}500,000 account for 81 percent of the revenue at stake in the regime, and reported profit falls when firms move to the profit base. Both the cost of the regime and the gain from narrowing it are therefore smaller than static estimates imply.

\section{Institutional background}\label{sec:inst}

The micro-enterprise income tax (\emph{impozitul pe veniturile microîntreprinderilor}), Title III of the Fiscal Code, replaces the 16 percent profit tax with a tax on revenue. It applies to companies whose revenue in the previous fiscal year was below a ceiling set in euro and that meet side conditions. Table~\ref{tab:regime} gives the ceiling and the conditions by year of revenue; Appendix~\ref{app:legal} gives the acts.

Three features matter. The first is timing. Under the in-year rule, a micro-enterprise whose cumulated revenue passes the ceiling during year $t$ owes profit tax from that quarter; for 2013 to 2015 it owed profit tax for the whole year. The ceiling is converted at the central bank (BNR) rate at the close of year $t-1$. Under the year-end rule, status for year $t+1$ depends on revenue at 31 December of year $t$, converted at the rate at the close of year $t$. When the ceiling is unchanged, the two rules put the notch a few thousand lei apart, and the in-year rule binds because it uses the earlier and lower rate. When a cut has been enacted for the following year, a firm faces two notches. That happened in 2022: Ordinance 16/2022 of July set \euro{}500,000 for 2023 status on 2022 revenue, and Law 370/2022 of 20 December deferred the revenue test to 2023 revenue. It happened again in 2025: Ordinance 156/2024 of 31 December 2024 set \euro{}250,000 for 2025 and \euro{}100,000 for 2026 status on 2025 revenue. A 2016 ceiling of \euro{}100,000, adopted on 27 October 2015 and published on 3 November, was tested on 2015 revenue. It is a further year-end notch, and we return to it below.

The second feature is the tax base. The ceiling is tested on the taxable base of Article 53. That base leaves out items such as subsidies, provision reversals and, from 2021, dividends received. The accounts report total revenue, which is the same or larger. Where the excluded items are not zero, a firm looks closer to the ceiling than it is, which shifts the bunching region slightly to the right in our data.

The third feature is the side conditions. The employee condition is met by a full-time contract, by part-time contracts adding up to one, or by a paid mandate contract. Linked enterprises are defined by holdings above 25 percent. The activities taxed at 3 percent regardless of revenue in 2024 and 2025 are software, hospitality, legal and medical services. The consulting cap was in force in 2014--2017 and, in a reformulated version, in 2023--2024; it is a margin the accounts do not show. The VAT exemption for small firms is a separate notch set in lei: 220,000 lei until March 2018, 300,000 from 1 April 2018 and 395,000 from 1 September 2025. Appendix~\ref{app:vat} treats it.

\section{Data}\label{sec:data}

The Ministry of Finance publishes, for each fiscal year, the main figures from the annual accounts of every company. Firms are identified by fiscal code (CUI) and CAEN activity code. The figures are balance-sheet totals, net turnover, total revenue, total expenses, gross and net profit or loss, and average headcount. The layout is stable from 2016; the 2014 and 2015 files use an older set of columns, which we harmonise. The 2021, 2023 and 2025 files were published a few weeks after the filing deadline, and the other files are later re-issues, so late filers are covered unevenly. Table~\ref{tab:panel} summarises the panel: 9.58 million firm-years after removing 32 duplicates, and 1.56 million firms. Total revenue is the variable for the micro-enterprise notch.

The accounts do not say which regime a firm is in. We recover it in two ways. The first is the tax pattern. Gross profit minus net profit is the income tax accrued. Divided by revenue, it is roughly 1, 2 or 3 percent for micro-enterprises; divided by pre-tax profit, it is roughly 16 percent for profit-tax firms. The second is the ANAF taxpayer registry, a snapshot dated 31 August 2023, which has one column per tax a firm is registered for. Table~\ref{tab:crosscheck} cross-tabulates the two for 2023. Of firms registered for the micro-enterprise tax, 84 percent show a micro pattern and 1 percent a profit-tax pattern. Of firms registered for profit tax, 22 percent show the profit-tax pattern and half paid no tax. Where both measures are decisive, which is the case for 57 percent of firms, they agree in 92.5 percent of cases. The tax pattern picks out micro-enterprises well and profit-tax firms poorly. We therefore use the registry flag where membership matters and the tax pattern as a check.

\section{Empirical strategy}\label{sec:method}

For each year $t$ the notch location is $L_t = T_t \, e_{t-1}$, where $T_t$ is the euro ceiling for revenue in year $t$ and $e_{t-1}$ is the BNR rate at the close of year $t-1$. Let $F_t$ be the distribution of revenue in year $t$ and $q_t = F_t(L_t)$ the quantile of the notch. For every other year $c$, let $L^*_c = F_c^{-1}(q_t)$ be the revenue at the same quantile. Year $c$ is an admissible control if no known notch of year $c$ lies within 25 percent of $L^*_c$; the known notches are the micro ceiling, any enacted or announced future ceiling, the \euro{}60,000 rate boundary and the VAT threshold. Bins are 1 percent of the location wide, in a window of $\pm$20 percent. The share $s_{t,k}$ is the count in bin $k$ divided by the count in the window, and the counterfactual share is the mean of $s_{c,k}$ over admissible controls. Excess mass is
\begin{equation}
b_t = \frac{\sum_{k \in B} \left( s_{t,k} - \bar{s}_{c,k} \right)}{\frac{1}{|B|}\sum_{k\in B} \bar{s}_{c,k}},
\end{equation}
where $B$ is the five bins just below the notch; $m_t$ is the same sum over the five bins just above. With 1 percent bins, $b$ is roughly the width of the bunching region in percent of the ceiling. The identifying assumption is that the shape of the revenue density around a given quantile is the same across years, apart from the notch. Quantile matching makes the counterfactual immune to inflation and to growth in firm size. It does not make it immune to changes in shape, so we test that: the estimator is run at 74 placebo locations, at 0.45 to 2.5 times each year's ceiling and at least 25 percent from any notch, where the true excess is zero. The notch list includes the year-end ceilings enacted for the following year. One of them, the \euro{}100,000 ceiling for 2016 tested on 2015 revenue, we found because an earlier placebo grid built without it had its largest value at that location. Because shares are normalised within the window, the excess and the missing mass offset by construction. We therefore also compute a count-based version, in which each bin is a share of all firms in the year, $N_t$; the integration constraint then becomes a test of where bunchers come from. Uncertainty is reported three ways: a multinomial bootstrap of bin counts, a block bootstrap that resamples control years, and the placebo distribution. Appendix Table~\ref{tab:controls} lists every control year with its matched revenue and its distance to the nearest notch. The closest cases are 26 to 34 percent, for the 2014--2016 notches and the two year-end notches. Tightening the rule to 35 percent changes the estimates from 2016 on by at most 0.01.

Three things the estimator does not deliver are stated once here. It does not locate the missing mass. The missing mass is spread over the window above the notch rather than concentrated next to it, so the standard conversion of excess mass to an elasticity has no anchor. The tax change on crossing depends on each firm's margin, because the base switches, so a representative-firm elasticity has no clear meaning. And reported margins themselves respond to the base, as Section~\ref{sec:base} shows. Appendix Table~\ref{tab:elasticity} reports the Kleven--Waseem reduced-form elasticity under two proxies for the bunching width; the two proxies differ by three orders of magnitude. We report excess mass and excess shares instead.

\section{Bunching at the ceiling}\label{sec:results}

\subsection{The spike follows the ceiling and the exchange rate}

Figure~\ref{fig:panels} shows a spike in the bin just below the ceiling in every year: at \euro{}65,000 in 2014 and 2015, \euro{}100,000 in 2016, \euro{}500,000 in 2017, \euro{}1,000,000 from 2018 to 2022, \euro{}500,000 in 2023 and 2024, and \euro{}250,000 in 2025. Figure~\ref{fig:lei} looks inside the five years at \euro{}1,000,000. Over those years the ceiling's lei value rose from 4.66 to 4.95 million as the leu depreciated, and the spike moved with it each year. Table~\ref{tab:lei} puts the modal 5,000-lei bin 1,400 to 5,600 lei below the ceiling at the previous year-end rate, which is the rate the in-year rule prescribes. The ceiling at the current year-end rate lies 79,000 to 115,000 lei further right in 2019 to 2021. Nobody rounds revenue to 4,779,300 lei, and a spike at that value that moves to 4,869,400 lei the next year is not an accounting convention.

\subsection{Difference-in-bunching estimates}

Table~\ref{tab:dib} reports the estimates, and Figure~\ref{fig:dib} shows the densities for four notches; all fifteen are in Appendix Figure~\ref{fig:dibfull}. In every year the treated density exceeds the same-quantile control density in the bins just below the notch, and the control density is smooth through the notch. Excess mass in the 5 percent below the notch ranges from 1.9 percent of the firms in the window, at the \euro{}500,000 ceiling of 2017 and the \euro{}250,000 ceiling of 2025, to 7.6 percent at the \euro{}500,000 ceiling of 2023. As a share of all filers it is 0.06 to 0.30 percent. Table~\ref{tab:robust} gives three checks. A block bootstrap over control years gives standard errors of 0.08 to 0.16. Dropping any one control year moves $b$ by at most 0.1. Narrowing the window to $\pm$10 percent lowers $b$ by 15 to 36 percent and the excess count by 12 to 31 percent; widening it to $\pm$30 percent raises both by about a tenth or less, except in 2014, where the VAT spike enters the wider window. That dependence is mechanical. Shares are normalised within the window, so a narrower window deflates every treated share by the bunchers' own weight in it. The count-based excess of Table~\ref{tab:countbased} does not use the window, and it is identical to within three firms at all three widths. Appendix Table~\ref{tab:robustextra} adds three more checks: control years before and after the treated year agree; correcting the control quantile for the bunchers changes $b$ by at most 0.1; and continuing firms give $b$ lower by 0.05 to 0.2.

The placebo distribution is the relevant benchmark. Across the 74 locations, $b$ has mean 0.01 and standard deviation 0.13; the 95th and 99th percentiles of $|b|$ are 0.27 and 0.34, and the maximum is 0.39. Every in-year estimate exceeds that maximum, most by a factor of two or more. The 2017 and 2025 in-year estimates, 0.76 and 0.73, are the weakest. In the count-based version the placebo maximum is 0.61, and those two estimates are 0.94 and 1.00, only one and a half times it. Two things dilute the 2025 notch: the enacted \euro{}100,000 ceiling for 2026 competed for attention, and at the 3 percent rate then in force the break-even margin was above the median buncher's margin. A within-year placebo points the same way as the grid. Hospitality firms were exempt from the ceiling in 2023, and that year they show an excess of under 1 percent of their window, against 7.6 percent for all firms (Table~\ref{tab:sector}).

Table~\ref{tab:countbased} gives the count-based version. At every large notch the treated year has more firms than the counterfactual across the whole 20 percent below the notch, not only in the five bins next to it. The deficit in the 20 percent above the notch is at most a seventh of the excess. The net difference over the window is 10 to 17 percent of the window for the 2019 to 2024 notches, against a placebo 95th percentile of 5 percent. Bunchers are therefore not drawn from the region just above the ceiling. It follows that the width of the bunching region, and any elasticity derived from it, is not identified in these data. Where the extra firms come from is less settled. There are three candidates. Firms below the ceiling may grow less, so that the density below it accumulates over the years the ceiling is in place; this is the build-up that \citet{kleven2013} document for Pakistan, and it shows here as a rise from 3.5 percent of the window in 2018 to 5.5 percent in 2021. New and split firms may enter the window; 11 percent of the 2023 excess is firms in their first year. Or the shape assumption may hold in shares but not in counts. Appendix Tables~\ref{tab:countwide} and \ref{tab:growth} give two checks. Extending the window to $\pm$50 percent finds, for 2019 to 2024, a surplus of 0 to 5 percent of the counterfactual in the first 10 percent above the notch, deficits of 1 to 8 percent in the 10 to 30 percent above, and nothing systematic further out. It also finds a surplus of 2 to 8 percent from 20 to 50 percent below the notch, further than a bunching response reaches, and for the 2014 to 2016 notches a deficit of 11 to 17 percent there. The count-based comparison therefore picks up differences in the shape of the distribution across years as well as the notch; the window normalisation removes exactly those differences. The growth check is closer to the mechanism. Take the notch years in which the ceiling was unchanged the following year. In every one of them, firms 5 to 20 percent below the notch grew less from $t$ to $t+1$, relative to firms 20 to 35 percent below, than the same bands did in control years; the gap is 0.3 to 2.6 points. In four of the six years the share crossing the next year's notch was 3 to 9 points lower. Slower growth below the ceiling is there, but it is small. The frictions reading of \citet{kleven2013}, a region above the notch where most firms do not move, fits the flat density above the notch as well. We read the surplus as partly accumulation and partly shape drift, and the conclusions do not rest on it.

Two further features of Table~\ref{tab:dib} deserve comment. The 2023 response is the largest, and it falls to 4.1 percent in 2024 at the same ceiling; in 2023 the ceiling halved and exit became permanent. Of the 2023 bunchers, 29.6 percent had been above \euro{}500,000 in 2022. Relative to a comparison group, that share is no higher than in years when that point was not a ceiling (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:transition}), so it is mean reversion, not displaced firms requalifying. The three extra rows show year-end notches operating, each with a different timing. Take 2015 first. The \euro{}100,000 ceiling for 2016 status was enacted in October 2015, with two months of the year left. Its lei value at the end-2015 rate is 452,450 lei. There is no excess below that value, but there is an excess of 0.71 (standard error 0.08) in the five bins above it, about 500 firms or 1.7 percent of the window, most of them in the first two bins. The 2014 distribution is flat there. The regime was mandatory. A firm below the ceiling that expected to lose from a 1 to 3 percent tax on revenue in 2016 could avoid it only by having 2015 revenue above the ceiling. Appendix Table~\ref{tab:escape2015} follows the two sides of the ceiling into 2016. Firms 0 to 3 percent above the ceiling were on profit tax the next year: 21 percent show a profit-tax pattern and 32 percent paid no tax. Among firms 0 to 5 percent below, the figures are 9 and 13 percent, and 57 percent show the micro pattern.\footnote{A fifth of the firms above the ceiling also show a micro pattern in 2016, when the law put them on profit tax. We read this as inference noise rather than as Article 53 exclusions moving their taxable base below the ceiling. In the 2014 cohort at the same quantile, every firm was above the \euro{}65,000 ceiling and on profit tax, yet 14 to 15 percent show the same pattern. The reason is that a profit-tax firm with a margin of 6 to 19 percent pays 16 percent of profit, which is 1 to 3 percent of revenue, and that is the signature the inference reads as the revenue tax. The exclusions may account for the remaining few points.} The median tax paid in 2016 was 0.5 percent of revenue above the ceiling and 1.0 percent below it. The firms in the spike have low margins, a median of 3.8 percent, and 60 percent of them are below the 6.25 percent break-even (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:yearend2015}); they gained from being above. What the panel does not show is a reversal. Their 2016 revenue grew by a median 4.2 percent, no less than the 2.5 percent of firms 3 to 10 percent above the ceiling. The share that fell back below the ceiling's value, 42 percent, is within a few points of that group and of the 2014 placebo cohort. One detail of the location supports the reading. While the year was still open, the monthly average rates were 4.42 lei in October, 4.44 in November and 4.50 in December 2015 \citep{bnr2015}, so \euro{}100,000 was worth 442,000 to 450,000 lei. The spike does not start there. It starts at 452,000 lei, just above the end-year value of 452,450 that fixed the test. Firms cleared the ceiling at its year-end value, whether by leaving a margin for the rate or by placing revenue once the rate was known. Neither round-number targeting nor fixed-lei targeting produces a spike that begins at 452,450 lei. The spike is firms that placed themselves on the profit-tax side of a ceiling they expected to lose from, and they stayed there. Whether the placement was a timing of real revenue or a reporting choice, the data cannot tell. Now 2022. A spike of 2.0 percent of the window appears at \euro{}500,000, the ceiling firms expected for 2023 status until the December deferral; there is none at that location in 2021. And 2025. A spike of 3.7 percent appears at \euro{}100,000, the ceiling enacted for 2026. At 1,000-lei resolution the spike runs from 494,000 to 500,000 lei. It straddles the \euro{}100,000 value at the end-2024 rate, 497,410 lei, which is why $m$ is positive for that row. The data cannot separate targeting of the last observable rate from targeting of 500,000 lei as a round number. For comparison, a polynomial counterfactual of the kind used by \citet{chetty2011} gives 0.4 to 2.0 at baseline, at bin widths of 1.5 to 2 percent of the ceiling and with the VAT spike inside the 2014 and 2015 windows. Across 27 combinations of bin width, degree and excluded window it gives anything from negative values to more than twice the baseline. The two sets of estimates are not on the same scale, and the comparison is only about stability.

\subsection{Who bunches}

The notch raises taxes only for firms whose margin exceeds the ratio of the two rates: 6.25 percent at a 1 percent revenue tax and 18.75 percent at 3 percent. Table~\ref{tab:margins} compares firms in the 5 percent below the ceiling with firms just above it, in the notch year and in the year before. The year before matters because Section~\ref{sec:base} shows that the base changes what firms report. Firms just above the ceiling in year $t$ crossed it during the year and closed their accounts on profit tax, so part of any gap in year-$t$ margins is the base effect rather than selection. In $t-1$ the two groups were on more similar bases; in 2017 and 2018 nearly all of them were on profit tax, because the previous ceiling was lower. The gap survives. Bunchers' median margins in $t-1$ are 7 to 20 percent, against 3 to 12 percent for firms just above. The share above the break-even margin in $t-1$ is 40 to 76 percent for bunchers, against 23 to 66 percent for firms just above. Table~\ref{tab:sector} shows where the bunchers are. In 2023, excess mass is 11 to 15 percent of the window in professional, administrative, health and real-estate services and in construction, and 10 percent in information services. It is 5 percent in trade, 3.5 in transport and 1 in agriculture. Bunchers in the service sectors report margins of 29 to 43 percent.

Table~\ref{tab:dibmargin} makes the split the unit of estimation. Firms are divided by their $t-1$ margin into those above the break-even margin for the rate at the ceiling and those at or below it. The estimator is run within each group, and each group is matched at its own quantile in the same control years. Bunching is concentrated where the tax wedge is. Among firms that gain, $b$ is 1.3 to 4.4 and the excess is 3 to 11 percent of the window. Among firms with a positive margin at or below break-even, $b$ is 0 to 1.3 and the excess is 0 to 3 percent. This resolves the puzzle of the 3 percent years. In those years the median buncher's margin, 13 to 17 percent, is below the 18.75 percent at which a 3 percent revenue tax saves tax, yet 2014 and 2015 show excess mass of 1.2 and 1.7. In 2015 the gaining group, a quarter of firms, has $b$ of 4.4 and an excess of 11 percent of its window; the low-positive group has 1.3 and 3 percent. In 2024 and 2025 the figures are 3.4 and 1.5 for the gaining group, against 0.6 and 0.2. The median hides a mix, and most of the bunching comes from the minority that gains. The third group is the informative one. Firms that reported a loss in $t-1$ would pay no tax at all if they crossed the ceiling. They bunch more than firms with a small positive margin: $b$ is 0.3 to 2.0 and the excess is 0.8 to 5 percent of the window, two or more standard errors from zero in nine of eleven years. Some of them will have expected a profit their previous year did not show. For the rest, no tax wedge holds them below the ceiling. This is the cleanest evidence in the paper for the mechanism \citet{harju2019vat} find at the Finnish VAT threshold, compliance cost. In those years, leaving the regime meant moving from a one-line tax base to quarterly profit computations, to the deductibility rules of the profit tax, and to the audit exposure that comes with them. In 2016 the rate depended on headcount and the median buncher paid 1 percent, so 2016 is not a 3 percent year.

\section{The base changes what firms report}\label{sec:base}

A micro-enterprise's tax does not depend on its reported profit; a profit-tax firm's tax does. Firms on profit tax therefore have a reason to report lower margins. They can book costs aggressively. Or they can pay the owner a salary rather than a dividend: the salary is deductible under profit tax and taxed on the owner, while the dividend is not deductible and was taxed at 5 to 8 percent. Both responses lower reported profit, and only the first is misreporting. The accounts do not separate personnel costs, so we cannot tell the two apart, and we say so. What we can do is measure the total.

\subsection{Design}

In 2018 the ceiling rose from \euro{}500,000 to \euro{}1,000,000. Firms with 2017 revenue between those values were moved from profit tax to revenue tax without doing anything, while firms above \euro{}1,000,000 stayed on profit tax. In 2023 the cut reversed the move. Wide bands, (\euro{}500,000, \euro{}1,000,000] against (\euro{}1,000,000, \euro{}2,000,000], compare firms that differ twofold in size, and they produce mean-reversion drift of up to 1.5 points in a placebo (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:eventstudy}). We therefore use bands 20 percent either side of the cut-off, assigned on two-year average revenue, in balanced panels. Placebos are built at the same time with the same bands at \euro{}1,500,000 and \euro{}2,000,000, where no regime changed. Table~\ref{tab:firststage} gives the first stage. In 2018 the treated band's micro share, inferred from the accounts, jumps from 15 to 55 percent. The control band just above the new ceiling goes from 15 to 29 percent, because some of those firms bunched under the ceiling. The control is therefore partly treated, which is why the entry effect fades over the horizon in the baseline. A control band of 1.2 to 1.5 times the cut-off stays at 15 to 25 percent. The exit design is fuzzier, and its first stage is not well measured. The inference classifies 42 percent of the control band as micro in 2022, although that band is above the ceiling; firms that cross the ceiling during the year pay both taxes and show a blended ratio. The ANAF registry records the regime a firm is registered for. It puts the treated band at 40 percent micro in August 2023 and 6 percent in June 2024, against 12 and 2 percent for the control band. By 2024, then, both bands were almost entirely on profit tax, and the treated band had lost the regime on a scale the inference understates. We therefore report the exit effect as an intention-to-treat estimate on the band that lost the regime, and we compute per-switch effects for the entry design only.

\subsection{Fiscal magnitudes}

Table~\ref{tab:fiscal} puts the regime in lei for 2023, using the ANAF registry to say who was in it. Micro-enterprises paid 3.3 billion lei of revenue tax. In every band below \euro{}500,000 that is 0.9 to 1.0 percent of their revenue, which is the rate the law prescribed. At 16 percent, their reported pre-tax profit would have yielded 10.7 billion, so the static gap is 7.3 billion lei. Of that gap, 81 percent comes from firms with revenue below \euro{}500,000. Only 12 percent comes from the \euro{}500,000 to \euro{}1,000,000 band, where the 2023 bunchers and the reclassified firms sit. Cash receipts bound the accrual from above rather than reconcile it. The Ministry of Finance's budget execution for 2023 \citep{mf2024} reports 5.49 billion lei for the state-budget chapter that carries the micro-enterprise tax together with withholding on non-residents and smaller items; the 2022 figure was 4.76 billion. Neither the execution note nor ANAF's statistical bulletin separates the micro-enterprise line. The chapter total is therefore an upper bound on cash receipts of the tax, and it cannot say how much of the gap to the 3.3 billion accrued in the statements is withholding and how much is coverage. The coverage side is the registry filter. The statement total covers the 455,035 firms that were registered for the micro-enterprise tax at 31 August 2023 and filed accounts. ANAF counts 495,448 registrations for the tax at 31 December 2023, against 912,875 a year earlier; these are registrations rather than filers. Firms that entered or left the regime during the year, including those the 2023 reform pushed out, are therefore outside the statement total. The registry matters here. The accounts-based inference would put the micro tax at 8.0 billion, because it classifies 10,000 firms above \euro{}1,000,000 as micro-enterprises. Those are large profit-tax firms whose credits and exemptions put their tax at 1 to 3 percent of revenue, and the registry puts 40,000 of the 45,000 firms in that band on profit tax. The gap is an upper bound, and the previous subsection says by how much. Firms moved to profit tax in 2023 reported margins 8 to 17 percent lower than their base. A static simulation that applies 16 percent to the profit those firms reported as micro-enterprises therefore overstates the revenue from reclassifying them by that share. \citet{ban2023} simulate revenue across ceilings from zero to \euro{}1,000,000 under exactly that assumption. The correction is first order for the reclassified band and second order for the regime as a whole, because the intramarginal firms, not the threshold, carry the cost.

\subsection{The employee condition}

From 1 January 2023 a micro-enterprise must have at least one employee, tested at 31 December 2022. The rule was enacted in July 2022. Table~\ref{tab:emp} shows the share of small firms with exactly one employee rising from 31.9 percent in 2021 to 37.6 in 2022 and 40.7 in 2023. Table~\ref{tab:emptrans} follows zero-employee firms. In 2022, 22.3 percent of them moved to exactly one employee, against 11.1 to 11.3 percent in the two preceding transitions; the figure is 20.3 percent when the sample is limited to firms present since 2020. Among zero-employee firms above \euro{}1,500,000, which the rule did not touch, the rate stayed at 5 to 6 percent. That is about 20,000 additional one-employee firms. Of the micro-enterprises still without employees at the end of 2022, 82 percent were registered for profit tax in 2023, and 15 percent had an employee by the end of 2023. The hiring came in the enactment year, and the rest left the regime. The aggregate shows the scale. ANAF's count of taxpayers registered for the micro-enterprise tax fell from 912,875 at 31 December 2022 to 495,448 at 31 December 2023, and to 368,900 at 31 December 2024 \citep[Table 2.2]{anaf2024, anaf2025}. The 2023 reform, that is the employee rule together with the halved ceiling, the three-firm rule and the consulting cap, took 46 percent of registrants out of the regime in one year. A further 26 percent had gone by the end of 2024; that fall mixes the 2024 rules with 2023 leavers whose deregistration was recorded only after the notification deadline of 31 March 2024. The registry counts registrations, not filers. It includes dormant firms and firms that never filed accounts, which is why it exceeds the 862,564 companies with 2022 accounts in our panel. The two series are not the same population, but the fall is the counterpart of Table~\ref{tab:emptrans}. The implied tax rates of the 2022 hirers suggest that many hired late in the year, but rate inference is noisy for one-employee firms, so we do not lean on the timing. Headcount counts a part-time contract as one. We therefore read this as compliance at the lowest cost rather than as job creation.

\subsection{Two further margins, in brief}

The same population bunches at the VAT exemption threshold, which is set in lei. Polynomial excess mass there is 2.8 to 6.6 in every year. \section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conc}

A ceiling that moved six times gives two things: a counterfactual density without a fitted curve, and the same firms reassigned between a revenue base and a profit base. The first shows that bunching at the ceiling is real. It follows the ceiling and the exchange rate, and it amounts to 2 to 8 percent of nearby firms and 0.06 to 0.30 percent of all firms, with no deficit just above the ceiling. Firms just below the ceiling grow a little less than firms further down, so part of the response is an accumulation below the ceiling; where the rest of the surplus comes from, the data do not settle. The second shows that reported margins rise when firms are moved onto the revenue base and fall by 8 to 17 percent of the margin when they are moved off it. Together they say that both the fiscal cost of the regime and the revenue from narrowing it are smaller than static estimates imply. Firms well below the ceiling carry 81 percent of the cost, and the profit those firms would report under profit tax is lower than the profit they report now.

Three design points follow, limited to the margins we measure. The ceiling is a lever on the intramarginal population, not on behaviour: moving it changes the behaviour of a few thousand firms and the bills of hundreds of thousands. The regime's advantage is concentrated in high-margin services. A structure that ties the rate to the margin, the minimum-tax design that \citet{sharma2025dual} show dominates threshold systems, would remove both the notch and the incentive to report margins differently on its two sides. Side conditions that can be met with a single contract produce that contract, as the employee rule did. Side conditions together with a halved ceiling removed 46 percent of registered micro-enterprises in a single year, 2023, a change in the regime's population larger than anything the ceiling alone has done. What the data do not settle is how much of the margin response is misreporting and how much is income shifting between salary and dividends. That needs personnel costs, which the public accounts do not carry.

\section{Data details}\label{app:data}

\paragraph{Regime inference.} The gap between gross and net profit also contains the specific tax on hospitality firms in 2017--2022 and other taxes on the result, net of sponsorship and cash-register credits. Ratios such as 0.8 or 2.4 percent therefore occur, and a firm with positive tax and a pre-tax loss is most likely, but not certainly, a micro-enterprise. Of firms registered for profit tax, 8 percent show a micro pattern and half paid no tax, which is what loss-making and dormant profit-tax firms look like. Of 2023 filers, 2.6 percent are not in the registry. In the registry's column dictionary, obligation 120 is the micro-enterprise income tax and obligation 100 is the profit tax.

\paragraph{Employees.} Beyond the blanks, headcount is reported as zero for a further 5 percent of small firms. Of the firms that went from zero to one employee in 2022, 12 percent paid the no-employee rate for most of that year; a true average would have rounded them to zero, so late-year hires are counted. The legal test is met by a full-time contract, by part-time contracts adding up to one, or by a paid mandate contract. The field distinguishes none of these.

\paragraph{Trade register.} The register lists administrators as of the snapshot date. It lists neither shareholders nor past administrators, and the open data has a representatives table only in snapshots from November 2025 onwards, so the administrators at the time of the 2018--2023 observations cannot be recovered. Company names carry no usable marker of sole ownership. Nearly all filers, 96 to 99 percent by year, link to at least one administrator, and the linked share is the same across the groups compared. Four percent of administrator records lack a birth date, so their key is the name alone; results are reported with and without them. Names, birth dates and addresses are used only to build hashed keys, and they appear in no output or replication file. Net turnover, the variable used for the VAT threshold, is blank for a fifth of micro-entity forms but for under 1 percent of firms near that threshold.

\section{The VAT threshold and the rate boundary}\label{app:vat}

The VAT threshold moved from 300,000 to 395,000 lei on 1 September 2025. In the 2025 distribution of turnover the spike at 300,000 lei is gone, and a spike at 395,000 has replaced it; its polynomial excess mass is 3.0, against 0.6 at that location in 2024 (Table~\ref{tab:vat}). Two checks follow from the 2024 rules, which limit a person to one micro-enterprise and test the ceiling on linked enterprises combined. The administrator premium disappears in 2024, at 1.4 percent for both groups, against 2.0 and 1.6 in 2023, while a gap remains on the address measure. But Table~\ref{tab:groups} shows no excess mass in the combined revenue of firms sharing an administrator at \euro{}500,000 in 2024, and firms inside such groups bunch individually at the same rate as single firms. Either the aggregation rule was not enforced or noticed in its first year, or common administration is too weak a proxy for the ownership links the rule is written on. Firms that lost the regime when the ceiling fell registered new firms at the same rate, relative to the band below, as placebo cohorts did.
